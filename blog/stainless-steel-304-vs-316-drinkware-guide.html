Every insulated bottle, tumbler and flask you import starts with one decision you rarely see on the price tag: the grade of stainless steel in the body. Most reusable drinkware is 304. A smaller, pricier slice is 316. Knowing the difference lets you stop overpaying for 316 where it doesn't matter — and stop under-specifying it where it does.

1. What the grade numbers mean

Stainless steel "grades" are alloy recipes. 304 is the workhorse of the drinkware world: roughly 18% chromium and 8% nickel, which is why you'll hear it called 18/8. That mix gives it bright finish, easy forming and good all-round corrosion resistance. 316 is 304 plus about 2–3% molybdenum. That single addition is the whole story — molybdenum sharply improves resistance to chlorides and many acids, the two enemies that make ordinary steel pit and stain.

Both are austenitic (non-magnetic in the annealed state, though cold-working from forming can leave harmless surface magnetism), both are food-safe, and both form the backbone of vacuum-insulated drinkware. The question is never "which is safe" — it's "which is the right spend for this product and market."

3. Corrosion resistance in real use

Spec sheets say "corrosion resistant," but the failures importers actually see come from three real-world conditions, not lab salt-spray tests:

  • Salt air and sweat. Bottles shipped through humid, salt-laden routes or sold in coastal markets are where 316 earns its keep. 304 is fine for most indoor use but can show tea-stain spotting near the sea.
  • Acidic and briny contents. Citrus, sports electrolytes, salted soups in a vacuum flask — 316 resists pitting that can eventually mark 304.
  • Cleaning and care. Both grades survive dishwashers, but leftover salts and cleansers left to dry in the seam accelerate any weak point. The lid, gasket and welded seam matter as much as the body grade.

For the vast majority of plain-water and coffee/tea products, 304 is the correct, cost-efficient choice. Reach for 316 when the use environment, not the marketing, calls for it.

4. Cost and when 316 pays off

Switching the body from 304 to 316 raises the steel cost, but the final unit-price gap depends on capacity, wall thickness, vacuum quality and finish — not the alloy alone. Treat 316 as a targeted upgrade, not a blanket upgrade.

Buyer's rule of thumb: specify 316 only where the environment justifies it. Paying 316 money across an entire lineup of plain-water bottles is the most common way importers quietly inflate cost with no shelf benefit.

5. Food-contact compliance

Grade choice and compliance are related but separate. Both 304 and 316 are food-grade austenitic steels; what customs and retailers check is the certificate trail, not the spoken grade. Confirm your supplier supplies:

  • Mill test certificate / material certificate stating the actual grade (304 or 316) of the body steel.
  • FDA (US) and LFGB (EU/Germany) food-contact certificates covering the finished product, including lid, gasket and straw, not just the body.
  • SGS / TÜV third-party test reports where your market or buyer requires them.
  • BPA-Free declaration for any plastic components.

6. FAQ

Is 304 stainless steel safe for drinking water?
Yes. 304 (often labeled 18/8) is a food-grade austenitic stainless steel and is the global default for reusable drinkware. It meets FDA and LFGB food-contact requirements when sourced from a certified mill and finished correctly.

When should I pay extra for 316?
Choose 316 only when the product faces salt, chlorides or aggressive acidic/briny contents — e.g. sports bottles for coastal or outdoor use, vacuum flasks for soups/salty beverages, or goods shipped through humid, salt-air routes and stored near the coast. For plain water and most coffee/tea, 304 is sufficient.

Will switching to 316 increase my unit cost a lot?
Body-steel cost typically rises in a single-digit to low-double-digit percentage range versus 304, but the final unit price also depends on capacity, vacuum quality and finish. We quote both grades itemized so you can decide on real numbers rather than a guess.

Are lids and gaskets also stainless?
Lids are usually 304 stainless or food-grade plastic (PP/TRITAN), and gaskets are food-grade silicone. The body grade (304 vs 316) is the main corrosion-decision; plastic and silicone parts carry their own FDA/LFGB certificates, supplied with every shipment.
