Choosing the Right Drinkware Capacity

The size of a bottle or tumbler looks like a design footnote — but it quietly decides your freight cost, your price point, your MOQ math and whether a product sells in a given market. This guide maps the common 350ml–2L tiers to real buying decisions.

Importers and brand owners usually pick a drinkware size based on what looks good in a render. That is the wrong starting point. Capacity is the single variable that ties together how a product feels in the hand, how much it costs to ship across an ocean, how many units fit in a carton, and which shelf it competes on. Get it wrong and you either pay for air you cannot sell, or ship a bottle nobody reaches for. Below is how to choose on purpose.

1. Why capacity is a sourcing decision

A bottle is mostly empty space surrounded by steel. That space is not free: it adds wall material, it adds carton cube, and it changes the perceived value of what the customer pays for. Three consequences follow for a buyer:

  • Freight is sold by the fuller of weight or cube. Lightweight vacuum bottles usually fill a container on volume before weight, so a bigger bottle mainly costs you space — fewer units per carton, more cartons per container.
  • Retail price bands are size-sensitive. A 350ml kids' bottle and a 1L sport bottle live in different price brackets and different aisles, even from the same mold family.
  • MOQ is shaped by shape, not by milliliters. The tooling that matters is the body shape and lid, not whether it holds 500 or 600ml. This means you can often widen a size range without widening the MOQ floor.

4. How capacity drives freight & landed cost

This is where size stops being aesthetic and starts being arithmetic. A 40ft high-cube container holds roughly 65–68 CBM. For lightweight vacuum drinkware, you will usually run out of cube long before you hit the ~26–28 ton weight limit. So the question is not "how heavy is one bottle" but "how many fit per carton and how many cartons per container."

A larger bottle needs a larger carton and more internal dunnage, so the units-per-carton drops. The net effect: a 1L program ships fewer sellable units per container than a 500ml program at the same mold cost. On a landed-cost model this can shift your per-unit freight from, say, a low figure to a noticeably higher one — not because the freight rate changed, but because you packed fewer paying units into the same space. We cover the full landed-cost math in our landed-cost guide.

Rule of thumb: before you expand a size range, ask the factory for the carton dimensions and units-per-carton for each size. Two sizes that look similar on paper can differ by 15–25% in units-per-container, which flows straight to your margin.

5. Capacity, MOQ & tooling

A common worry is that a bigger bottle means a bigger minimum order. In practice, capacity is rarely the lever. What sets the MOQ is:

  • Body shape — a stock shape reuses existing tooling; a bespoke shape needs a new mold.
  • Lid & accessory — a custom lid or straw is costlier to tool than the body volume.
  • Decoration — printing setup and color changes drive batch minimums.

Because of this, a 500ml and a 700ml bottle from the same stock family often sit in the same MOQ band (commonly 500–1,000 pcs per SKU for ODM). The practical takeaway: you can build a 3-size range from one mold family and keep the MOQ floor low, which is exactly how most private-label brands start. 6. Volume vs heat / cold retention

Capacity also interacts with performance. A larger vacuum body has more thermal mass and a smaller surface-area-to-volume ratio, so it generally holds temperature longer than a tiny one — but it is also slower to cool a hot drink to a drinkable temperature. For cold retention the effect is purely positive: bigger = colder longer. For hot drinks, a 1L flask keeps coffee hot past lunch, while a 350ml tumbler cools into the "ready to sip" zone faster. When you write the product spec, state the retention test (95°C water, measured at 6h and 12h) per size rather than quoting one number for the whole range — our insulation standards guide explains the method.

7. A practical selection framework

If you are launching or extending a line, use this order:

  1. Pick the hero size from the channel, not the catalog. Commute/daily → 450–550ml. Gym/sport → 650–900ml. Outdoor → 750ml–1L.
  2. Add one secondary size only if it serves a different user. A 350ml kids' bottle next to a 500ml adult bottle is a real second buyer; a 520ml next to a 500ml is not.
  3. Get carton data for every size before signing, and run the landed-cost model per size.
  4. Keep the range on one mold family for the first 12 months to protect the MOQ floor and simplify QC.
  5. Validate with local retail scanning in your target market before scaling beyond three sizes.

8. FAQ

What is the most common drinkware capacity for private label?
The 500ml (17oz) bottle and the 500–600ml tumbler are the most common starting points for private-label programs because they balance daily-use practicality, acceptable per-unit weight for freight, and broad retail appeal. Smaller 350ml kids' bottles and larger 750ml–1L sport/office bottles are the next most frequent.

Does a larger bottle cost more to ship?
Yes, but not linearly. A larger capacity adds steel weight and carton volume, so both actual weight and cube grow. For lightweight stainless vacuum bottles the container usually fills on cube before weight, so adding capacity mostly costs space, not a higher freight rate — but it can reduce how many units fit per carton.

Does capacity change the MOQ?
Capacity alone rarely moves the MOQ floor; the shape and lid do. Most factories set MOQ by tooling and decoration, not by milliliters. A stock-shaped 500ml and a stock-shaped 1L bottle from the same mold family often share the same MOQ band (commonly 500–1,000 pcs per SKU). Confirm exact figures per inquiry.

Should I launch with one size or a size range?
For a first order, one hero size (often 500ml) plus one secondary size (e.g. 350ml kids or 750ml sport) is a low-risk way to test the market. A full 4–6 size range is better once you have sales data, because every size is a separate SKU with its own packaging, barcode and MOQ.
