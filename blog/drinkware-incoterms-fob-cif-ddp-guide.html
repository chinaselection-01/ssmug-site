Two suppliers quote the same insulated tumbler at the same unit price. One says "FOB Ningbo", the other says "DDP Hamburg". Only one of those numbers is comparable to your budget — and choosing the wrong term can add double-digit percentage points to landed cost. Incoterms are not legal fine print; they are the single most under-negotiated line in a drinkware contract. This guide explains the three terms importers meet most and how to use them without overpaying.

1. Why Incoterms decide your real cost

Incoterms are a published set of trade rules — the current edition is Incoterms 2020 — that define three things in every international order: who pays each cost (freight, insurance, loading, clearance, duty), where risk of loss transfers from seller to buyer, and who handles export and import formalities. The unit price of a bottle says almost nothing on its own; the Incoterm attached to it determines which costs are already inside that price and which ones will arrive as separate invoices later.

For drinkware this matters more than most categories. A shipment of vacuum flasks is bulky, relatively low in value per cubic metre, and often consolidated across many SKUs. Freight and destination charges can rival the product cost itself. An importer who negotiates hard on unit price but accepts a vague shipping term frequently hands back those savings at the port.

2. The 11 rules — and the 3 you'll use

Incoterms 2020 contains eleven rules, but a drinkware importer will meet only a handful. They split into two groups: sea and inland-waterway terms (FOB, CFR, CIF, FAS) and any-mode terms (EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP). Most bottle and tumbler orders move by ocean container from Ningbo or Shanghai, so the sea terms dominate — but the any-mode terms appear when buyers consolidate into air freight or cross-border trucking.

  • FOB (Free On Board) — the default for ocean freight from China. Seller loads the goods on board at the named port; buyer takes over from there.
  • CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight) — seller also pays ocean freight and minimum insurance to the named destination port, but risk still passes at origin.
  • DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) — seller delivers to a named place in your country with import duty and taxes handled. The most convenient term for the buyer, and the most demanding for the seller.

Industry-typical practice for custom drinkware out of Zhejiang is to quote FOB Ningbo or FOB Shanghai; exact terms are always confirmed per inquiry rather than assumed.

The table reveals the trade-off. FOB keeps freight in your hands — you nominate the forwarder, negotiate the rate, and see every cost. CIF bundles freight and basic insurance into one number but removes your control of the carrier. DDP is the most hands-off: one landed number, no import paperwork on your side — useful for new importers, but the convenience is priced into the seller's margin, and the seller must be able to clear goods in your country.

4. Where cost and risk transfer

The most misunderstood point: paying for freight is not the same as carrying the risk. Under both FOB and CIF, risk of loss or damage transfers to the buyer the moment the goods are on board at the origin port. CIF only adds the seller's obligation to pay freight and buy minimum cover (commonly Institute Cargo Clauses (C) or equivalent). If a container is damaged at sea under CIF, the buyer claims on the insurance — not on the seller. This is why experienced importers buying higher-value drinkware add their own all-risks cargo policy even when the seller provides basic CIF cover.

With DDP, by contrast, risk stays with the seller until the goods are delivered at the named place, and the seller carries import clearance. That is genuinely more protective — but you pay for it, and it only works where the seller can legally and practically import into your market.

Key distinction: Incoterms allocate cost and risk — they do not set payment terms, transfer title, or change your inspection rights. Your deposit schedule, quality clause (such as an AQL standard) and compliance documents are separate contract terms and should be written down regardless of which Incoterm you choose.

5. The hidden costs in a "cheap" quote

When you compare a low FOB offer against a higher DDP offer, make sure you are comparing like with like. Line items that frequently sit outside a headline FOB price include:

  • Destination charges — terminal handling, documentation and port fees at the arrival port.
  • Customs brokerage — fees for filing the import entry, separate from duty itself.
  • Import duty and VAT/GST — driven by the HS code and destination market, not by the supplier.
  • Insurance — basic cover under CIF is minimal; many buyers top it up.
  • Inland delivery — from port to your warehouse or a fulfilment centre.
  • Do-not-forget — consolidation or deconsolidation fees when your drinkware shares a container with other products.

A transparent supplier will give you an itemized quote and, on request, a DDP-equivalent estimate so you can model both. Treat a single unexplained "all-in" number as a question to ask, not a bargain to accept.

6. How to write Incoterms into a PO

A safe clause names the rule, the place, and the edition. "FOB Ningbo, Incoterms 2020" and "CIF Rotterdam, Incoterms 2020" are unambiguous; "FOB China" or "shipping included" are not — they invite disputes over where risk transfers and who pays destination charges. Always put the named port or place after the term, and specify the edition year.

Before you approve the proforma invoice, confirm four things in writing: the exact Incoterm and named port/place; the payment schedule (a deposit with balance against shipping documents is a common industry structure); the inspection standard; and which compliance files accompany the shipment (for food-contact drinkware, certificates for both steel and plastic components such as lids and gaskets). Our own quotations state FOB Ningbo or FOB Shanghai by default and can be reworked to CIF or DDP on request — the point is that you choose with full information, not by accident.

7. FAQ

What does FOB Ningbo mean for a drinkware order?
FOB (Free On Board) Ningbo means the seller delivers the goods on board the vessel at Ningbo port and covers all costs up to that point, including export clearance. Risk transfers to the buyer once the goods are on board. The buyer pays ocean freight, insurance, destination charges, import duty and inland delivery. FOB Ningbo and FOB Shanghai are the most common quotations for drinkware shipped from Zhejiang.

Is CIF better than FOB for an importer?
Neither is inherently better. Under CIF the seller pays ocean freight and minimum insurance to the named destination port, but risk still transfers at the origin port. FOB gives the buyer control of the carrier and freight negotiation, which usually lowers landed cost for experienced importers. Ask for both and compare.

What is the difference between DDP and DAP?
Under DDP the seller delivers with import duty and taxes paid and handles import clearance. Under DAP the seller delivers to the named place but the buyer handles import clearance and pays duty and taxes. Many suppliers avoid DDP because they cannot control the buyer's import licensing.

Which Incoterm should I use for a first drinkware order?
For a first order, FOB Ningbo or FOB Shanghai with a freight forwarder you trust is the most transparent option. If you want a single door-to-door number, request a DDP quote but ask for the FOB breakdown alongside it. Confirm the term and named port in writing on the proforma invoice and PO.
