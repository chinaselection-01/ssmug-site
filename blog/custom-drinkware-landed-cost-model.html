Most first-time drinkware importers budget from a single number — the FOB unit price a factory quotes — and discover the real cost only when the container reaches their dock. The gap between "factory price" and "cost to have a sellable unit in your warehouse" is where programs go unprofitable. This report gives you a reproducible model so the surprise happens on paper, before you commit.

1. Why the FOB price is only half the story

A manufacturer's quote is almost always FOB (Free On Board): it covers the product, its retail or plain packaging, factory QC, and delivery of the loaded goods onto the ship at the named port. For our shipments that is normally FOB Ningbo or FOB Shanghai. Everything after the ship's rail — ocean freight, marine insurance, destination port charges, customs clearance, duty, VAT, and trucking to your door — is on you.

That split matters because drinkware is a low-weight, high-cube product. A 500 ml steel bottle might weigh 300 g but ship in a carton that occupies far more space than its weight justifies. So you rarely hit a container's weight limit; you hit its volume limit first. The freight component of your landed cost is therefore driven by how cleverly you pack, not by how heavy the product is.

2. The landed-cost formula

Build every quote on the same skeleton. Missing any one line is how budgets blow up:

Cost layerWhat it coversTypical driver
Product (FOB)Goods, packaging, factory QC, delivery to portModel, steel grade, finish, decoration
Ocean freightPort-to-port sea transportCube and weight; LCL vs FCL rate
Marine insuranceCoverage for loss/damage in transitUsually ~0.3–0.5% of CIF value

Customs clearanceBroker fee, duty, VAT/GSTHS code, country of import
Last mileTrucking from port to your warehouseDistance, pallet count
ContingencyDemurrage, re-work, sample airfreightPlan 3–5% of FOB

Landed cost per unit = (FOB + freight + insurance + destination handling + duty + VAT + last-mile) ÷ sellable units, plus a contingency buffer. Note that duty and VAT are usually calculated on the CIF value (FOB + freight + insurance), not on FOB alone — a detail that quietly inflates the bill if you model duty on the factory price.

3. Freight: LCL vs FCL, and why cube beats weight

You have two ways to move goods across the ocean:

  • LCL (Less than Container Load): your pallets share a container with other shippers. You pay only for the cube/weight you use. Good for trial orders of a few hundred kilos, but the per-cubic-metre rate is higher and you absorb consolidation/deconsolidation handling.
  • FCL (Full Container Load): you rent the whole box — 20ft, 40ft, or 40ft High Cube. The rate is fixed regardless of fill, so the unit freight cost collapses as you approach full utilization. A 40ft HQ holds roughly 68–76 cbm depending on carton shape.

Because drinkware is light, a 40ft HQ rarely reaches its ~26-ton weight ceiling before it reaches its cube ceiling. The practical rule: design cartons to maximize cube fill, then consolidate enough SKUs to fill the box. A container that is 60% full on cube is paying 40% freight for air.

4. Import duty & VAT by market

Duty depends on the precise HS code (drinkware typically falls under HS 9617 for vacuum flasks and HS 3924 / 6911 / 7323 for other vessels) and the importing country's schedule. The ranges below are indicative only — always confirm the exact rate and any trade-remedy measures (anti-dumping, Section 301) with a licensed broker before you promise a margin.

Rule of thumb: in most markets the VAT/GST you collect and remit will dwarf the duty you pay. If you are VAT-registered and reclaim it, model the cash impact, not the headline rate. If you are not, that tax is a real cost of goods.

5. The hidden line items buyers forget

These are the lines that do not appear on a factory quote but appear on your bank statement:

  • Carton and pallet upgrades for long-haul stacking — cheap insurance against crushed goods.
  • ISF / ENS / AMS filing (security manifests) required before the vessel sails to the US or EU.
  • Demurrage and detention if your warehouse cannot take delivery fast enough after arrival.
  • Re-inspection or re-work when a container fails destination QA.
  • Airfreight for samples or a stock-out — a tempting fix that costs 5–10× sea freight.
  • Currency spread on deposits and balances if you settle in a currency other than the quote currency.

7. FAQ

What does the FOB price actually include?
FOB covers the product, its packaging, factory QC and delivery of the goods onto the vessel at the named port (typically FOB Ningbo or FOB Shanghai for our shipments). It does NOT include ocean freight, insurance, import duty, VAT, destination port handling, or last-mile delivery.

How much of my budget should I reserve for freight?
For drinkware, ocean freight plus insurance and destination handling commonly runs 15–35% of FOB on a consolidated LCL shipment, and 8–18% on a full FCL container. The share rises on small, light orders where you pay for unused cube. Always quote freight per your actual cube and weight.

Is import duty calculated on the FOB or the CIF value?
Most customs authorities assess duty on the CIF value (Cost + Insurance + Freight) or transaction value that includes freight and insurance to the port of entry. VAT, where applicable, is then charged on CIF plus duty. Confirm the valuation basis with your broker for the exact HS code.

What is the single biggest lever to lower landed cost?
Cube utilization. Drinkware is bulky but light, so you usually run out of container space before weight. Designing products and cartons to pack tighter, and consolidating SKUs into one FCL, typically saves far more than shaving the factory unit price by a few cents.
