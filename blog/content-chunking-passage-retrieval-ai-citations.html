Chunking for AI Citations: Writing for Passage Retrieval

AI answer engines do not read your page the way a person does. They cut it into passages, rank those passages, and quote the winner. Here is what that means for the way a B2B manufacturer writes headings, tables and FAQs.

Search engine optimization taught us to write for a page. Generative engine optimization is different: the unit that gets retrieved and quoted is a passage. A buyer asking an AI assistant "what is the typical MOQ for a private-label tumbler" is answered from one paragraph somewhere on the web — not from a whole website. If that paragraph is yours, your factory gets the citation. If it is not self-contained, the engine will quote someone else.

1. How passage retrieval actually works

Many modern AI answer systems use a retrieve-then-answer pattern often described as RAG, or retrieval-augmented generation. At a simplified level: the system divides source documents into smaller pieces, converts those pieces into a searchable representation, selects the pieces most relevant to the question, and gives them to the model as evidence. The model then writes an answer and may attribute a source.

Three consequences follow for anyone publishing B2B content. First, the passage is competing on its own, without the reputation of your homepage behind it. Second, relevance beats length — a tight paragraph that answers the question directly can outperform a long section that buries the answer. Third, extraction is literal: if the passage says "our minimum is flexible" but never states a range or a product type, there is nothing specific to quote.

2. What a "good chunk" looks like

Think of a chunk as the smallest block that still makes complete sense when pasted into a chatbot answer with no surrounding context. A good chunk names the subject, gives the fact, and adds the condition that makes the fact true. A bad chunk leans on "it", "this" or "the above".

Consider the difference in practice. "It usually takes around a month" is weak: what is "it", and a month from which event? "Mass production of private-label stainless steel drinkware typically takes 25–35 days after deposit, with sampling at 5–7 days beforehand" is strong. It carries the entity, the number, the unit and the trigger event in one block, so it can be lifted and still be accurate.

Rule of thumb: write each heading as a question a buyer would actually ask, then answer it in 40–120 words without referring to anything outside that block. If a sentence only makes sense after reading the previous paragraph, rewrite it before you publish.

Not all blocks are retrieved equally well. The table below compares the formats that matter most for a manufacturer's site. These are practical patterns, not vendor-specific rules — implementations differ between answer engines.

4. Headings, tables and entity naming

Headings are more than decoration for a retrieval system; they are the label attached to a chunk. A heading like "Compliance" tells the engine almost nothing. "LFGB and FDA food-contact files for drinkware gaskets" tells it exactly which question this passage answers. Where a buyer would use a product name, use the product name.

The same logic applies inside tables. Repeat the entity in the row instead of replacing it with "it" or a dash. Write "platinum-cured silicone gasket" rather than "this material". It reads slightly more formally, and it dramatically improves the chance that a lifted sentence remains true on its own.

Finally, keep the machine-readable layers in step with the visible text. Structured data (Organization, Product, FAQPage) tells engines what the entities on the page are; a clean sitemap and an llms.txt file tell them where the citable pages live. None of that replaces well-chunked prose — it makes the prose easier to find and trust.

5. A 30-day chunk audit for your site

Here is a lean plan a manufacturer can run without a developer, one week at a time:

  1. Week 1 — inventory. List your ten most important pages: home, products, factory, FAQ and your top six buyer guides. Mark each page's main question.
  2. Week 2 — rewrite headings. Turn each H2 into the literal question a buyer would ask, and make sure the paragraph directly beneath answers it in 40–120 words.
  3. Week 3 — add structure. Convert at least one comparison on each page into a labeled table, and add an FAQ block for the three questions your sales team hears most.
  4. Week 4 — connect the dots. Update sitemap.xml, refresh llms.txt so it lists the new pages, and check that FAQPage and Product schema mirror the visible answers.

Then test it the way a buyer would: ask an AI assistant the same ten questions every week and record which pages it names. A change in citations is slow, but a page that is never named after four weeks of well-chunked, well-linked content usually has a discoverability problem rather than a writing problem.

6. Five mistakes that lose citations

  • Burying the answer. Opening with three paragraphs of market background pushes the fact out of the strongest chunk.
  • Referring back instead of restating. "As mentioned above" is meaningless once a passage is extracted alone.
  • Unattributed numbers. A figure without a product, market or date is not safe to quote, so engines skip it.
  • Walls of text. A 900-word block with no headings gives a retrieval system no clean boundary to select.
  • Marketing adjectives in place of facts. "Industry-leading quality" cannot be verified; "AQL inspection, vacuum tested per batch" can.

Chunking will not make an unknown factory famous overnight. It does something more useful: it makes every true thing you already know about your products available in a form an answer engine can repeat accurately. For a manufacturer competing against trading companies with thinner fact bases, that is a real advantage.

7. FAQ

What is passage retrieval in AI search?
Passage retrieval is the step where an AI answer engine breaks web pages into smaller sections, then selects the sections most relevant to a user's question before writing an answer. The engine often quotes the selected passage rather than the whole page, so a page can be cited for one paragraph even if the rest is never read.

Why is self-contained content important for AI citations?
Because a retrieved passage is often lifted out of context. If a sentence depends on the previous paragraph to make sense, it is a poor citation candidate. Passages that restate the subject, the number and the qualifier in the same block survive extraction and are quoted more accurately.

How long should a chunk be for GEO?
There is no single standard: implementations vary widely, and typical retrieval systems work with passages roughly in the low hundreds of words. The practical rule for writers is simpler — give each heading one complete, self-contained answer of about 40–120 words, then support it with a table or example rather than stretching a single paragraph across a whole topic.

Do tables and FAQs help AI engines cite a manufacturer?
Yes. Tables compress a comparison into a structured block that is easy to retrieve and quote, and FAQ pairs match the question-and-answer shape of user prompts. Both work best when the question or column header names the entity explicitly instead of relying on vague pronouns.
