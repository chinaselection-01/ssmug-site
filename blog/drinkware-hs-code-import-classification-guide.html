HS Codes for Drinkware Imports

Which customs heading covers a vacuum flask, a stainless tumbler, a plastic bottle or a multi-tier lunch box? A practical guide to the codes, the construction details behind them, and the paperwork that keeps a shipment moving.

Most drinkware disputes at customs are not about price. They start with a product description that is too vague, a code copied from a similar-looking item, or an invoice that disagrees with the sample. This guide explains how vacuum flasks, tumblers, plastic bottles and food containers are commonly classified, and what a buyer should ask a factory before the goods are packed. The six-digit headings below are the international HS baseline; national schedules add digits, and your broker should confirm the final code for your destination market.

1. Why the code matters more than the unit price

The HS code printed on the commercial invoice follows the goods all the way through clearance. It determines which duty rate and tax treatment applies, whether a trade measure or quota is triggered, and how the product is described to the customs authority. A two-cent saving on the factory quote can turn into a costly hold if the declared code does not match the physical product in the carton.

There is a second reason to get this right early. Classification is iterative: a broker asks for a material breakdown, the factory replies with a marketing description, and the importer is left to reconcile the two. When that exchange happens before production, it is a five-minute conversation. When it happens after the vessel sails, it can mean a rewritten invoice, a reissued packing list, or storage charges. Treat classification as part of product development, not as a paperwork task handed to the forwarder at the last minute.

One boundary is worth stating plainly: a factory can provide accurate facts about what it makes, but it cannot guarantee the import code. The importer of record is responsible for the declaration, and the customs authority in the destination country makes the final determination. Any number a supplier gives you is an informed starting point.

2. The four headings you will meet

Drinkware orders rarely need more than a small set of headings. The table below describes the common international baseline. National tariff lines extend these codes, so use it to frame the conversation with your broker rather than as a final declaration.

9617.00Vacuum flasks and other vacuum vessels, complete with casesVacuum bottles, insulated tumblers and food jars with a vacuum layer

Two products that sit next to each other on a shelf can therefore land in different headings. A steel tumbler with a welded vacuum body and a steel tumbler with a single wall may look almost identical in a catalog photograph, yet they are described differently for customs. Keep the physical construction at the center of the conversation.

3. The decisive question: vacuum or not?

For insulated drinkware, this single question does most of the work. A vacuum vessel has two walls with the air removed from the space between them; that gap is what slows heat transfer. If the product has that construction, it is commonly described as a vacuum vessel under 9617.00, whether the outer shell is stainless steel or the capacity is 350 ml or two litres. If the product is single-wall steel, foam-insulated, or simply a thick plastic cup, the relevant heading changes.

Buyers often inherit a code from an earlier order and reuse it across a whole catalog. That is risky when the range mixes construction types. A supplier with a broad line — our own catalog covers 167+ models across bottles, tumblers, flasks and food containers — will have different construction families in the same shipment, and each family should be described on its own line of the invoice. Asking for a model-by-model material and construction sheet before the order is booked is the simplest way to avoid a single blanket description that fits none of the products.

Do not be tempted to choose the lower-duty code because two items look alike. Misdescription is a compliance problem for the importer, and the physical sample is the reference the inspector will use. If your supplier cannot tell you whether a tumbler is vacuum-built, that is a signal to request an exploded view or a cross-section photo before you pay a deposit.

4. Material, sets and mixed components

Once the construction is clear, material determines the subheading. The steel grade is worth recording but rarely changes the six-digit heading: 304 and 316 are both stainless steel. What matters more for customs is the material of the body, and for food-contact compliance it is the material of every part that touches the drink — lid, gasket, straw and interior coating.

A common point of confusion is the lid. A silicone gasket or a Tritan straw does not normally reclassify a stainless vacuum bottle, because the vessel gives the product its essential character. The exception to watch is a genuine set: a bottle packed with a separate glass insert, a lunch kit with several distinct containers, or a promotional pack combining drinkware and non-drinkware items. Sets should be discussed with the broker, because local rules decide whether they are declared together or as separate lines.

Ask your supplier for a bill of materials by component, not a one-line statement that the item is "stainless steel". A useful breakdown names the body grade, the vacuum construction, the lid polymer, the gasket material, the straw material if fitted, and any interior coating. It doubles as the basis for your food-contact file, which should include FDA and LFGB documentation where relevant. A factory operating under ISO 9001 with SGS and TÜV reporting should be able to produce this without hesitation.

6. Paperwork that keeps everyone aligned

Classification is only consistent if the documents agree with each other. The commercial invoice should use the same product description as the spec sheet, and the HS code should match the one your broker intends to declare. The packing list should identify model numbers so a carton can be traced back to a line on the invoice. For multi-model containers, a simple cross-reference of model, carton count and invoice line prevents the most common mismatch.

Alongside those commercial documents, keep the technical file close at hand: material declarations, food-contact test reports, and the approved sample record. If a question arrives during clearance, a factory that can answer within a few hours is worth far more than a slightly cheaper quote. Our own production is quoted FOB Ningbo or FOB Shanghai, with sample development in 5–7 days and mass production in 25–35 days, which leaves a comfortable window to settle classification questions before goods leave the plant.

Finally, check origin marking requirements for the destination market independently of the HS code. Marking rules are a separate obligation, and a correct tariff line does not excuse a missing or incorrect country-of-origin mark on the product or retail box.

7. Five mistakes that cost importers money

The same small errors recur across categories. Avoiding them costs nothing and removes most of the delay risk:

  • Copying a code from a look-alike product. Shape does not determine classification; construction and material do.
  • Using one code for an entire mixed catalog. Vacuum and non-vacuum models in one container should be described separately.
  • Leaving plastic parts out of the compliance file. A gasket or straw that touches the drink needs its own food-contact documentation.
  • Waiting until the cargo is on the water. Classification questions are cheap to answer before production and expensive after shipment.
  • Treating the supplier's number as a guarantee. The importer owns the declaration; the destination customs authority decides.

None of this requires a customs lawyer. It requires a spec sheet detailed enough that the product can be identified without opening the carton, and a supplier willing to share the facts behind it. 8. FAQ

What HS code is used for vacuum-insulated drinkware?
Vacuum flasks and other vacuum vessels complete with cases are commonly classified under HS heading 9617.00, regardless of whether the body is stainless steel. Destination schedules may extend this to 8 or 10 digits, so confirm the full code for your market with your broker.

Does a non-vacuum stainless steel tumbler use the same code?
Usually not. A single-wall or non-vacuum stainless steel tumbler is generally treated as an article of stainless steel for table or kitchen use, commonly under heading 7323.93, while a vacuum-insulated tumbler falls under 9617.00. The construction, not the silhouette, is what matters.

Does the 304 or 316 steel grade change the HS code?
No. Both 304 and 316 are stainless steel, so the grade does not move the product to another heading at the six-digit level. The grade does matter for material declarations, corrosion claims and food-contact documentation, so state it accurately on the invoice and spec sheet.

Can a supplier guarantee the import HS code?
A factory can supply an accurate product description, material breakdown, construction detail and supporting test reports, but classification is the importer's responsibility and the final decision rests with the destination customs authority. Treat any supplier code as a starting point, not a guarantee, and have your broker confirm it before shipment.
