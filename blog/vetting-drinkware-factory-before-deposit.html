The Yongkang–Jinhua belt in Zhejiang produces the bulk of the world's vacuum-insulated drinkware, which is exactly why it attracts two very different kinds of seller: factories that own the molds, furnaces and printing lines — and brokers who resell someone else's production. A deposit paid to the wrong one is the fastest way to lose a season. This guide is the checklist we wish every first-time importer ran before transferring money.

1. Why due diligence comes before the deposit

A deposit is not a payment for goods; it is a bet on a counterparty. Once it leaves your account, your leverage collapses. The work of separating a real factory from an intermediary takes a few hours and a handful of well-chosen questions — far cheaper than a 30% prepayment on a container that was never going to ship on spec. Due diligence is not about distrust; it is about price discovery. A factory that prints its own lids can quote a different number than one that subcontracts the same step, and only the first can defend that number when you ask.

2. Document check: license & certificates

Start with paperwork that cannot be faked easily. Ask for the unified social credit code and the business license, then confirm the registered business scope includes manufacturing (not only "trading" or "e-commerce"). Export-capable factories hold an import–export license and can name the ports they ship from — for our region that is typically FOB Ningbo or FOB Shanghai.

On compliance, do not accept a logo screenshot as proof. Request the actual test reports:

CertificateWhat it provesWhy it matters
ISO 9001A documented quality-management systemBaseline for consistent production, not a product test
FDAFood-contact suitability (US)Required for the US market
LFGBGerman food-contact standardStrongest EU acceptance; stricter than the baseline
SGS / TÜV reportsThird-party material & migration testsIndependent credibility on the actual lot
BPA-FreeNo bisphenol-A in plastic partsNeeded for lids, gaskets and straws, not only steel

Critical detail: certificates for the plastic components — lid, gasket, straw — must travel with the steel certificates. Many importers check the bottle body and forget the lid, then fail a port inspection on the silicone.

3. Confirming real production capacity

A capacity claim is only real if you can see it. The single most reliable remote check is a live video walk of the floor: steel stock, vacuum brazing or welding furnaces, coating/printing, and final assembly. A factory with 500,000+ units per month of capacity will have dedicated lines, not a corner bench.

Two questions cut through most claims:

  • "Show me the mold for this shape." In-house mold making is the difference between a factory and a reseller. It also determines how fast a design change turns around.
  • "Which steps do you subcontract?" Every factory outsources something. The honest answer ("we print in-house, we buy gaskets") tells you more than a perfect one.

4. Financial & trade-reference check

Longevity is a proxy for stability. A factory founded in 2017 has survived a trade-war cycle and a pandemic freight crisis — that track record is worth more than a glossy catalogue. Ask for two verifiable trade references in markets similar to yours (a European buyer, a Middle-East distributor) and actually email them. A genuine factory will introduce you happily; a broker will stall.

Also confirm the export footprint. Suppliers serving 50+ countries have the documentation muscle (commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, compliance dossier) that first-time exporters fumble. That back-office competence is what keeps your container moving at the port.

5. The sample test that actually tells you something

Sampling is usually 5–7 days, and a refundable sample fee is a healthy signal — factories confident in their quality refund it against your first bulk order. But a sample only proves the sample. Make it earn its keep:

  • Leak test the lid under pressure, not by eye.
  • Heat-retention test: fill with 95°C water, measure at 6h and 12h, compare against the claim.
  • Dishwasher / abrasion check on the decoration — laser and powder-coat survive; cheap stickers do not.
  • Food-contact smell test: a neutral, odorless interior is the baseline for any certified product.

6. Red flags table

No single item is fatal, but a cluster is. Score the supplier before you wire anything:

SignalHealthyRed flag

Rule of thumb: any two red flags in the same column means you pause. A real factory competing for your order will happily clear every item on this list — because it costs them nothing they don't already have.

7. On-site vs remote verification

You do not need to fly to China to vet a supplier, but you should know what each method can and cannot prove:

MethodCostWhat it confirms
Video call walkFreeFloor exists, basic capability
Third-party inspection (pre-shipment)Low–moderateQuality, count, packaging at shipment
On-site audit (SGS/Bureau Veritas)ModerateSystem, capacity, compliance in depth
Trade-show meeting (e.g. HOMELIFE, Canton)TravelFace-to-face, samples in hand

For a first order, a video walk plus a paid pre-shipment inspection covers most risk at the lowest cost. Reserve the full on-site audit for programs above a certain annual volume.

8. FAQ

How do I confirm a drinkware supplier is a real factory, not a trading company?
Ask for the unified social credit code and business license, request a live video walk of the production floor (molding, vacuum brazing, printing, assembly), and verify they hold manufacturing, not just trading, scope. A real factory can show you steel stock, vacuum furnaces and in-house printing — a broker cannot.

Which certificates should a qualified drinkware factory hold?
At minimum ISO 9001 for quality systems, plus food-contact certificates appropriate to your market: FDA and LFGB are the most commonly accepted. SGS and TÜV test reports add credibility. Compliance files for plastic parts (lid, gasket, straw) must be supplied alongside the steel certificates.

How long does a factory sample take, and should I pay for it?
Sampling is typically 5–7 days for stock shapes. A refundable sample fee is normal and a good signal — factories confident in their quality usually refund the sample cost against your first bulk order. Treat a "free sample with no process" offer with caution.

What is the single biggest red flag before paying a deposit?
A supplier that refuses any production-floor evidence, has no verifiable export history, quotes far below market with vague specifications, or pushes for a large upfront deposit with no milestone-based payment terms. Combine at least two of these and walk away.
